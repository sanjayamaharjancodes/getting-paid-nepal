Receiving USD via Bank SWIFT Wire Transfer in Nepal

For a large, one-off payment from a direct client — not a stream of small freelance-platform payouts — a direct wire straight into your Nepali bank account can work well. It also comes with a paperwork risk that catches people off guard: the bank has to correctly classify what the money is for, and getting that wrong causes real problems. This page explains what we've found, and what's still genuinely unclear.

Note on this page: This page does not carry a Payoneer or other affiliate link — banks don't typically run freelancer referral programs, so there's no commission angle here. We're covering it because it's a real, legal rail worth understanding, not because it monetizes this site.

How it works, in outline

A direct international client can instruct their bank to wire USD to your Nepali bank account via the SWIFT international payment network. Nepal's licensed commercial banks ("A-class" banks) are equipped to receive SWIFT wires — this is routine, established banking infrastructure, not something exotic. The part that requires care is not the wire mechanics themselves, but how the receiving bank codes the inflow.

The classification risk — the part most guides skip

According to Nepal industry reporting we reviewed, a documented, recurring problem is that inbound USD for IT/freelance services sometimes arrives at the bank labelled as a personal remittance rather than payment for services — and once that happens, it can be difficult to have it correctly recognized as service-export income after the fact. [Reported by Nepal industry/trade press; we treat this as a well-corroborated but not primary-source-verified pattern — confirm your own situation with your bank.]

The practical takeaway: before you ask a client to wire you a meaningful amount, ask your bank's trade-services or foreign-exchange desk directly whether the inflow will be booked as export-of-service proceeds against an invoice — not as a personal remittance. Nepal's central bank, Nepal Rastra Bank (NRB), separates these two categories at a regulatory level: service-export receipts fall under a different reporting framework than personal/worker remittances. Getting classified correctly, from the start, is much easier than trying to fix it later.

What the bank may ask for

Based on the account-opening and trade-documentation patterns we found in publicly available guides, expect your bank to want some combination of the following when a meaningful international wire is inbound:

  • A copy of the invoice or contract the payment relates to
  • Your PAN (tax registration) details
  • Basic identification / proof of who you are and what the income relates to

The exact requirements vary by bank and by the size/nature of the transfer — this is genuinely a "call the bank first" situation, not a fill-in-the-blanks checklist we can give you with full confidence. [conf: medium — derived from account-opening document patterns across multiple Nepal banking guides, not confirmed with a specific bank's trade desk for individual freelancers specifically.]

The certificate you may want to ask about

Nepali banks can issue a certificate confirming that a foreign-currency inflow was received and realized against a specific invoice or purpose — commonly referred to in the region as a Foreign Inward Remittance Certificate (FIRC) or similar bank realization document. If you may need to prove the source of foreign income later (for tax filing, or for a loan/visa application, for example), ask your bank whether they issue this kind of certificate and request one at the time of the transfer, rather than after the fact. [conf: low-medium — the certificate concept is well established regionally; we have not independently confirmed the exact Nepal-specific certificate name or process with a bank's trade desk.]

Which banks

Nepal's larger commercial banks generally have trade-services or foreign-exchange desks that handle this kind of inflow, including banks such as Standard Chartered Bank Nepal, Nabil Bank, NIC Asia Bank, and Global IME Bank, among others. We are not recommending one specific bank — the right choice depends on where you already bank, the desk's responsiveness, and account fees, which vary. If you're opening an account specifically for this purpose, call ahead and ask the trade-services desk the classification question above before you commit.

A regulatory environment that's actively changing

Nepal's foreign-exchange rules for export proceeds have been amended more than once recently — reported changes include adjustments to how repatriation approvals are processed and to the paperwork format used for export-proceeds reporting (part of it reportedly moved toward digital submission rather than only paper forms). [conf: medium — reported by Nepal legal/industry press; primary NRB circular text not independently opened by us.] The practical implication for you is simple: don't treat any specific procedural detail on this page as permanent. Confirm current requirements with your bank before a large transfer, especially if it's been a while since you last checked.

A note some sources mention: export proceeds are sometimes described as expected to be repatriated (brought back to Nepal) within a certain window, reportedly around six months, after which additional bank reporting can be triggered. We found this described mainly in a goods-export context, and it's unclear to us whether or how it applies to individual service/freelance income specifically. [conf: low — single-context source; ask your bank if this is relevant to your situation.]
